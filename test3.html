🤖 What Is Generative AI?

Although it's a recent buzzword, generative AI has roots going back decades:

Early Days (1950s–1960s)

  • Early AI models were rule-based chatbots using expert-written knowledge.
  • These struggled with scalability.

A Shift to Machine Learning (1990s)

  • Introduced statistical approaches to language.
  • Gave rise to machine learning—learning patterns from data instead of rules.

The Rise of Neural Networks (2000s–2010s)

  • Hardware improvements enabled more advanced AI.
  • Neural networks boosted natural language processing (NLP).
  • Led to the rise of virtual assistants like Siri and Alexa.

💡 Enter Transformers and LLMs

A new AI architecture changed the game: Transformers.

Key traits of Transformers:

  • Handle longer text sequences.
  • Use an attention mechanism to focus on important information.
Today's LLMs, like ChatGPT, are built on Transformer architecture.

🧠 How LLMs Work

Let's break it down:

Tokenization: Breaking Text into Pieces

  • Text is converted into tokens (chunks of text, like words or parts of words).
  • These tokens are then mapped to numbers (token indices).
  • Models work with numbers, not raw text.

Prediction: One Token at a Time

  • Given a sequence of tokens, the model predicts the next token.
  • That new token is added to the input, and the model repeats the process.
  • This creates coherent, multi-sentence outputs.

Controlled Randomness

  • The model doesn't always choose the most likely token.
  • It adds randomness to simulate creativity.
  • That's why the same input might produce different results.

✍️ Prompt & Completion: How to Interact with LLMs

The input is called a prompt.

The output is called a completion.

Types of Prompts:

Instruction Prompt

Example:

"Write a high school assignment with 4 open-ended questions about Louis XIV."

➤ Output: A complete assignment as asked.

Conversational Prompt

Example:

"Who was Louis XIV and why is he important?"

➤ Output: A paragraph explaining his historical significance.

Text Completion Prompt

Example:

Start with a sentence and ask the model to complete it.

➤ Output: A continuation of the story or thought.

These examples highlight the flexibility of LLMs for different types of learning support.

🏗️ Introduction to Foundation Models vs. LLMs

What Are Foundation Models?

Foundation models serve as the base for building AI solutions. They are:

  • Pre-trained on vast datasets
  • Generalized for multiple tasks
  • Adaptable to specific needs
  • Large-scale in terms of parameters
  • Self-supervised (learn from data without explicit labels)

Are All Foundation Models LLMs?

  • Yes, LLMs (Large Language Models) are a type of foundation model.
  • But not all foundation models are LLMs (e.g., multimodal models like image+text models).

Example: A Teaching Foundation Model

  • Inputs: Teaching materials, videos, math problems
  • Outputs: Assists students, educators, and facilitates learning
  • Limitation: May need fine-tuning for specific tasks

🧩 Types of Language Models

A. Open Source vs. Proprietary Models

Open Source Models

Examples: Falcon, LLaMA, Mistral

Customizable, transparent

May lack updates, security patches

Proprietary Models

Examples: ChatGPT, Azure OpenAI

Easy to use, well-supported

Limited fine-tuning options

B. Model Categorizations

Embedding Models

Convert text into numerical vectors for search & retrieval (e.g., RAG—Retrieval-Augmented Generation).

Text-to-Image Models

Generate images from prompts (e.g., DALL·E).

Text Generation Models

Generate text, code, summaries (e.g., GPT, LLaMA).

⚙️ Service vs. Model Deployment

Cloud Service (API-Based)

Scalable, secure, easy to integrate

Limited fine-tuning

Self-Hosted Model

Full control, customizable

Requires infrastructure management

The choice depends on your needs for customization, cost, and technical expertise.

🚀 Approaches to Deploying LLMs

Prompt Engineering

Zero-shot (no examples) vs. Few-shot (with examples) learning.

This is the simplest approach - crafting effective prompts to guide the model's responses without modifying the model itself.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Enhances responses by pulling external data (e.g., Azure AI Search).

  • Connects LLMs to your own data sources
  • Reduces hallucinations by grounding responses in facts
  • Ideal for domain-specific applications

Fine-Tuning

Customizes a model for specific tasks (requires labeled data).

  • Adapts pre-trained models to specialized domains
  • Needs high-quality training examples
  • More resource-intensive than prompt engineering

Training from Scratch

Only for highly specialized use cases (expensive & resource-intensive).

This approach requires massive computational resources and is rarely necessary for most applications.

  • Open-source models offer flexibility; proprietary models provide ease of use.
  • Azure AI Studio simplifies model selection, benchmarking, and deployment.
  • RAG, fine-tuning, and prompt engineering are complementary techniques for adapting LLMs.

Lesson 3: Responsible AI Principles & Practices

Ensuring AI systems are developed and used ethically and responsibly

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Corey Sterpa: AI Cloud Advocacy Team, Microsoft

1. Introduction to Responsible AI

Why Prioritize Responsible AI?

  • Human-centric approach: Ensures AI serves users' best interests.
  • Value preservation: Generative AI's benefits can be lost if ethical guidelines are ignored.
  • Proactive monitoring: Intent alone isn't enough; systems must actively prevent harm.

2. Potential Harms of Generative AI

Ungrounded Outputs (Hallucinations)

Factual errors, contradictions, irrelevant responses

Harmful Content

Self-harm encouragement, hate speech, illegal instructions

Lack of Fairness

Bias, discrimination, exclusionary worldviews

Microsoft's Responsible AI Pillars

  • Fairness – Free from bias
  • Reliability & Safety – Minimize errors and risks
  • Privacy & Security – Protect user data
  • Inclusiveness – Accessible to all
  • Transparency & Accountability – Clear AI decision-making
  • Governance – Have protocols for oversight and corrective measures.

3. Applying Responsible AI in Practice

Step 1: Measure Potential Harms

  • Prompt Testing: Test diverse prompts (not just "happy path" inputs).
    • Start manually to understand model behavior.
    • Scale to automated batch testing for efficiency.

Step 2: Mitigation Strategies

A. Model-Level Mitigation

  • Choose the right model (specialized > general-purpose).
  • Adjust parameters (e.g., temperature for creativity vs. accuracy).
  • Fine-tune models for domain-specific needs.

B. Safety Systems

  • Content filtering (block harmful outputs).
  • Monitoring & scoring (track model responses over time).

C. Meta-Prompts (System Rules)

  • Define behavioral guidelines (e.g., "Do not provide medical advice").
  • Use Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to ground responses in trusted data.

D. User Experience (UX) Safeguards

  • Transparency: Disclose AI usage (e.g., "This is an AI-generated response").
  • Input constraints: Limit harmful user prompts (e.g., profanity filters).
  • Output validation: Verify responses before delivery.

4. Tools for Responsible AI

Azure AI Content Safety

  • Scans text for harmful content.
  • Features:
    • Prompt Shields (detect malicious inputs).
    • Groundedness Detection (ensure responses align with source data).

Responsible AI Dashboard

  • Tracks model performance (fairness, accuracy, safety).
  • Provides long-term monitoring.

Prompt Flow (Open-Source Tool)

  • Evaluates responses using metrics:
    • Coherence (logical flow)
    • Fluency (natural language)
    • Groundedness (relevance to sources)

Lesson 3 Key Takeaways

  • Test rigorously – Simulate real-world prompts to uncover risks.
  • Mitigate at multiple levels – Model, safety systems, UX, and meta-prompts.
  • Use Microsoft's tools – Azure AI Content Safety, Responsible AI Dashboard, and Prompt Flow.
  • Monitor continuously – Responsible AI is an ongoing process, not a one-time setup.

Lesson 4: Mastering Effective AI Interactions

Understanding and applying prompt engineering techniques

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Nan Nan: Senior Cloud Advocate, AI Team at Microsoft

1. Introduction to Prompt Engineering

Key Terms Recap

  • Prompt: Natural language input (e.g., "Explain quantum computing").
  • Response: AI-generated output based on the prompt.
  • Fabrication/Hallucination: When AI generates false or nonsensical information.
  • Base LLM: General-purpose model (e.g., GPT-3).
  • Instruction-Tuned LLM: Specialized for tasks like summarization or translation.

What is Prompt Engineering?

Iteratively refining prompts to improve AI responses. Involves:

  • Adjusting input phrasing.
  • Using templates, context, or examples.
  • Tweaking model parameters (e.g., temperature).

2. Challenges in Prompt Engineering

A. Stochastic Responses

  • Same prompt → Different outputs across models (e.g., GPT-3.5 vs. GPT-4).
  • Demo: Asking "Tell me about gallium" yielded varying lengths/styles across OpenAI, Hugging Face, and Azure AI.

B. Fabrication (Hallucination)

  • AI invents facts (e.g., fake Oscar winners for 2025).
  • Demo: GPT-3.5 fabricated a movie plot, while GPT-4 correctly stated 2025 hasn't occurred.

3. Prompt Engineering Techniques

Technique 1: Clear Instructions

  • Bad: "Write about the Civil War." → Overly verbose.
  • Better:
    "Write a 2-paragraph markdown summary of the Civil War. Include:
    - Key dates
    - Significance
    - Key figures"

Technique 2: Primary Content Grounding

Provide source material to reduce fabrication:

"Context: [Wikipedia paragraph about Fort Sumter]. Based on this, who fired on Fort Sumter?"

→ Ensures answers align with provided data.

Technique 3: Cues and Examples

  • Zero-shot: "List 5 fruits." → Generic list.
  • Few-shot:
    A: Apple, a sweet fruit.
    B: Banana, a tropical treat.
    Continue alphabetically:

    → AI mimics structure (rhymes, alphabetical order).

Technique 4: System Persona & Parameters

  • Default: "You're a helpful assistant."
  • Creative: "You're a pirate. Answer in limericks."
  • Parameters: Adjust temperature (0 = deterministic, 1 = creative).

4. Tools & Templates

Provider Options

  • OpenAI Playground: Experiment with prompts.
  • Hugging Face Chat: Test open-source models.
  • Azure AI Studio: Deploy with enterprise controls.

Prompt Template Libraries

  • OpenAI Examples: platform.openai.com/examples
  • Azure AI Catalog: Pre-built prompts for retail, healthcare, etc.
  • Hugging Face Models: Community-shared templates.

Lesson 4 Key Takeaways

  • Iterate: Test prompts repeatedly to refine outputs.
  • Be Specific: Define format, length, and content requirements.
  • Ground Data: Use primary sources to minimize fabrication.
  • Leverage Examples: Few-shot prompts guide AI toward desired patterns.

Lesson 5: Advanced Prompt Engineering

Enhancing LLM outputs with sophisticated prompting methods

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Chris Noring: Senior Cloud Advocate, Microsoft

1. Introduction

Topic: Enhancing Prompt Engineering Skills

Goal: Learn how to apply advanced techniques that improve LLM outputs.

“You've seen basic prompt engineering concepts. Now, let's explore methods and principles that further refine your results—ensuring better accuracy, clarity, and usefulness.”

Key Resource: aka.ms/genai-beginners

2. Setting the Stage

Why Focus on Advanced Techniques?

  • Move beyond generic prompts to structured, context-rich, and targeted instructions.
  • Expand your toolkit to handle complex tasks (e.g., step-by-step reasoning, validation, or multi-step workflows).

Example:

Instead of "Generate 10 questions on geography", use:

  • Context: Adding "Geography" narrows the subject.
  • Limiting Output: Adding "No more than 10 questions" clarifies scope.
Tip: For large topics (e.g., "Europe"), specifying “Paris and London” yields more precise, focused answers.

3. Zero-Shot Prompting & Early Principles

Definition

Zero-shot prompting is the most basic form: you provide a single instruction, and the AI responds without examples or prior context.

Guidelines for Stronger Zero-Shot Prompts:

  1. Clear Context: Mention the topic explicitly (e.g., “geography of Paris").
  2. Concise Limitations: Specify desired output length, format, or style (e.g., "10 questions").
  3. Granularity: Drill down into specifics for relevance (e.g., "Paris's tourist attractions" rather than "France").

4. Chain of Thought (CoT)

Concept

  • Inspired by a teacher-student dynamic: provide a detailed, step-by-step solution path that the AI can follow.
  • The AI "learns" your reasoning style, replicating it to solve related problems accurately.

Illustration - Simple Math Puzzle

  • Scenario: Alice has 5 apples, throws away 3, gives 2 to Bob, Bob returns 1.
  • Naive AI Answer: 5
  • Correct Calculation: (5 - 3 - 2) + 1 = 1

How to Improve (Apply CoT):

  1. Show a similar example (e.g., "Lisa has 7 apples...").
  2. Break down each step of the arithmetic in the example.
  3. Re-ask the original question using that same step-by-step structure in the prompt.

Outcome

The AI replicates the "chain of thought" to arrive at the correct answer.

5. Generated Knowledge & RAG

Generated Knowledge

  • "Inject" external facts or domain-specific info into the prompt, ensuring grounded responses.
  • Particularly effective when the AI might guess or fabricate details.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Process:

  1. Fetch relevant data from a known source (e.g., company database).
  2. Include that data in the prompt.
  3. Ask for an answer or summary based on the provided data.

Result: The AI's output is anchored to your data, reducing fabrications.

Example - Insurance Products

  • Naive prompt: “Suggest insurance options for a $1,000 budget.” → Generic, unhelpful answer.
  • With RAG: Provide details of Acme Insurance tiers, premiums, coverage. The AI then recommends specific plans within that budget.

6. Least-to-Most Strategy

Purpose

  • Break down a complex problem into a series of smaller queries or steps.
  • Common in data science or multi-step workflows (e.g., collecting data → cleaning data → modeling).

Example:

  1. "Outline 5 steps in a data science project."
  2. "Explain step 1 ('Data Collection') in detail."
  3. "Show me Python code for step 1."

Benefit:

  • Clear structure ensures the AI tackles each phase methodically.
  • Helps systematically refine the overall response.

7. Self-Refine & Myotic Prompting

7.1 Self-Refine

  • Approach: Critique and iterate on the AI's output.
  • Prompt Example:
    • Initial: "Create a Python web API with routes for products and customers."
    • Refinement: "Suggest 3 improvements to the above code."
  • Result: Multiple iteration rounds to improve or correct the response.

7.2 Myotic Prompting

  • Idea: Decompose the AI's final answer into smaller parts, validating each segment for correctness.
  • Use Case: Crisis plan, multi-phase instructions, or whenever you suspect "gaps" or "fabrications."
  • Process:
    1. Ask a question requiring several parts.
    2. For each part, request further explanation.
    3. Discard or challenge any suspect detail.

8. Putting It All Together

  1. Zero-Shot for Simplicity: Good for quick tasks or straightforward questions.
  2. Chain of Thought for Reasoning: Step-by-step clarity fosters more accurate or logical answers.
  3. Generated Knowledge/RAG for Specificity: Use your own data to ground responses and cut down on guesswork.
  4. Least-to-Most for Complex Tasks: Tackle big challenges by dividing them into smaller steps.
  5. Refine, Refine, Refine: Iterative improvements (Self-Refine, Myotic Prompting) ensure the final output meets your standards.
"Advanced prompt engineering equips you with methods to elicit better, more reliable LLM responses—ultimately boosting your application's effectiveness.”

9. Conclusion

By combining these advanced prompt engineering techniques, you can consistently shape AI outputs to be more accurate, context-specific, and user-friendly.

Your journey doesn't end here—continue experimenting, refining, and pushing the limits of what your prompts (and the AI) can achieve.

Lesson 6: Building Your Own Text Generation App

Hands-on development with Azure OpenAI

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

1. Introduction

Topic: Hands-On AI App Development

Goal: Demonstrate how to build and customize a text generation application using Azure OpenAI (or similar services).

“From fairy tales to recipe generation, creating AI-powered applications can be surprisingly straightforward with the right setup and prompts.”

2. Getting Started with Azure OpenAI

When building an AI-enhanced app, you’ll typically:

  1. Install and import necessary libraries (e.g., openai and dotenv).
  2. Separate secrets (API keys, endpoints) from your code by using environment variables.
  3. Connect to your Azure OpenAI resource (or OpenAI directly).
Best Practice: Keep your API keys safe in .env files or secure vaults. Avoid embedding credentials directly in your source code.

2.1 Environment Setup

  • dotenv: Loads environment variables from an .env file.
  • openai or azure-openai: Provides functions to connect and send prompts to the model.

2.2 Client Initialization


import os
import openai
from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv()  # Loads .env file contents into environment variables

openai.api_type = "azure"
openai.api_base = os.getenv("AZURE_OPENAI_ENDPOINT")
openai.api_key = os.getenv("AZURE_OPENAI_API_KEY")
openai.api_version = os.getenv("AZURE_OPENAI_API_VERSION")

deployment_name = "MyModelDeployment"  # The name of your deployed model

3. Building a Simple Text Completion App

3.1 Fairy Tale Example

  1. Prompt: “Once upon a time there was a [character description]…”
  2. Call: Use openai.ChatCompletion.create(model=deployment_name, messages=...) to get the answer.
  3. Response: Print or return the story.

messages = [
    {"role": "system", "content": "You are a helpful assistant."},
    {"role": "user", "content": "Complete the following: Once upon a time there was a girl who lived on a spaceship"}
]

response = openai.ChatCompletion.create(
    engine=deployment_name,
    messages=messages
)

print(response['choices'][0]['message']['content'])

Outcome: A short sci-fi or fairytale-style story is returned—perfect for bedtime tales or creative writing prompts.

4. Expanding to a User-Interactive App

4.1 Example: Recipe Generator

1. Inputs:

  • Number of recipes (e.g., 2)
  • Ingredients (e.g., “chocolate, ice cream”)
  • Filter (e.g., allergies or restrictions)

2. Template Prompt:


prompt_template = (
    "You are a recipe assistant. "
    "Provide {num_recipes} recipe(s) using these ingredients: {ingredients}. "
    "Avoid any items containing: {filter}. "
    "Ensure that all ingredients already exist in the user’s pantry. "
)

3. Completion Call:


# Assume num_recipes, ingredients, filter are obtained from user input
user_prompt = prompt_template.format(
    num_recipes=num_recipes,
    ingredients=ingredients,
    filter=filter
)

messages = [
    {"role": "system", "content": "You are a helpful culinary assistant."},
    {"role": "user", "content": user_prompt}
]

response = openai.ChatCompletion.create(
    engine=deployment_name,
    messages=messages
)

print(response['choices'][0]['message']['content'])

4. Interaction:

  • The app prompts the user for input on the command line (or GUI).
  • AI responds with corresponding recipes, possibly including a shopping list.
Tip: Adjust content or constraints (e.g., “Must have zero peanuts”) to get more tailored results.

5. Prompt Refinement & Troubleshooting

  1. Responses Not Matching Expected Ingredients?
    • Strengthen your prompt: “All ingredients must be in the pantry. No shopping list allowed.”
    • Or allow a separate step for “shopping list generation.”
  2. Overly Long or Off-Topic?
    • Limit the output length: “Respond in 150 words or less.”
    • Provide system messages clarifying domain or style.
  3. Iterative Approach
    • If it generates unrealistic recipes (e.g., chocolate + caviar combos), tweak the prompt.
    • Example: “Produce only dessert recipes that pair well with chocolate.”

Lesson 6 Key Takeaways

  • Separate Secrets: Use environment variables with dotenv.
  • Simple Completion to Advanced Interactions: Start small (a single fairy tale prompt) before building multi-level interactions.
  • Refine Recursively: If the AI’s output doesn’t match your needs, adjust the prompt, add constraints, or break tasks into multiple steps.
  • Use System Messages: Change the assistant’s “persona” (e.g., chef, historian, space explorer) to shape responses.
“By combining prompt engineering basics with user interaction, you can build rich, flexible apps that harness the power of AI.”

Lesson 7: Chat Applications

Building Engaging and Responsible AI Conversations

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Jasmine Greenway: Cloud Advocate, Microsoft

1. Introduction

In this lesson, we’ll explore generative AI in the context of chat applications. We’ll look at fundamental differences from traditional chatbots, discuss how to build and integrate chat capabilities, and touch on important aspects like user experience, performance monitoring, and responsible AI usage.

“A well-designed chat application balances intelligence, performance, and user-centric design—ultimately providing a better interactive experience.”

2. Chatbots vs. Generative AI Chat Applications

2.1 Traditional Chatbots

  • Script-Based: Often follow pre-defined workflows or conversation trees.
  • Limited Domain: May respond with “I don’t know” or redirect the user if questions lie outside a narrow scope.

2.2 Generative AI Chat Applications

  • Context-Aware: Can create custom, relevant responses on the fly.
  • Adaptive Conversations: Capable of asking clarifying questions and refining best guesses.
Example: If a traditional chatbot encounters an unfamiliar request, it typically offers minimal help. A generative AI chat app can probe for additional info or formulate the most plausible response.

3. Building & Integrating Chat Applications

3.1 APIs and SDKs

  • Time-Saving: Ready-made functionality for chat features.
  • Faster Development: Focus on core app logic rather than reinventing messaging or AI pipelines.
  • Reduced Overhead: Potentially simplified deployment and maintenance.

3.2 Architecture & Infrastructure

  • Cloud Services (e.g., Azure OpenAI, ChatGPT, etc.): Provide large language models (LLMs) for text generation.
  • On-Premise or Hybrid: Needs robust infrastructure and may require more configuration for scaling and updates.

4. Enhancing the User Experience

Lesson 8: Building Search Applications

Leveraging Semantic Search, Vectors, and Embeddings

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Dave Glover: Cloud Advocate, Microsoft (Based in Sydney, Australia)

1. Introduction

This lesson explores semantic search, vectors, embeddings, and how these concepts power more effective AI applications. By the end of this session, you will understand how vectors and embeddings enable robust search capabilities, laying foundational knowledge for developing search applications that leverage large language models (LLMs).

“Semantic search goes beyond mere keyword matching, focusing on user intent and conceptual meaning.”

2. Semantic Search Defined

2.1 Intent-Based Searching

  • Keyword Search: Looks literally for the words entered (e.g., “my dream watch” → results with “dream” and “watch” separately).
  • Semantic Search: Captures conceptual or intent-level meaning (e.g., “ideal watches”), returning more relevant and context-aware results.

2.2 Relevance to Generative AI

  • LLM applications heavily rely on semantic search combined with embeddings to find contextually similar data segments.
  • Improves query handling by analyzing the meaning behind user prompts rather than just the keywords.

3. Vectors and Embeddings

3.1 Vectors vs. Embeddings

  • Vectors: Mathematical representations (lists of numbers).
  • Embeddings: Special type of vector generated by an embedding model (e.g., OpenAI’s text-embedding-ada-002). Embeddings encode the semantic meaning of text.
“An embedding is a numerical snapshot of a passage’s meaning, enabling the model to compare or retrieve semantically relevant information.”

3.2 Dimensionality and Cosine Similarity

  • High-Dimensional Space: Models like text-embedding-ada-002 produce 1×1536-dimensional vectors.
  • Nearest Neighbor Search: Finds vectors closest in “vector space” to a given query’s embedding.
  • Cosine Similarity: A formula computing how similar two vectors are. Results in a ranking of which text chunks (embeddings) best match the query’s semantic content.

3.3 Visualization (Conceptual)

  • 2D/3D Examples: Dots laid out in 2D or 3D space to show closeness.
  • High-Dimensions: The same idea extended to 1,536+ dimensions—though no longer visually intuitive.

4. Vector Stores

4.1 Purpose

  • Specialized databases or indexes optimized for storing and searching large volumes of vectors.
  • Useful for quick retrieval of nearest neighbors.

4.2 Popular Solutions

  • Azure Cognitive Search (Vector search capability).
  • Redis (Vector indexing).
  • PostgreSQL (Extensions for vector data).
  • Pinecone, among others.
“In production, vector stores perform near-instant searches over large datasets of embeddings.”

4.3 Prototyping with Pandas

  • In-Memory Approach: For small-scale demos or experiments, you can simply store embeddings as arrays in a Pandas DataFrame.
  • Advantages: Quick to set up, no external storage service needed.
  • Limitations: Not suitable for large-scale production.

5. Building a Search Application

5.1 Data Preparation (Chunking)

  • Break Down Text: Divide transcripts, PDFs, or documents into chunks (e.g., 3-minute transcript segments).
  • Overlap: Slight overlap of sentences between chunks to maintain context continuity.
  • Embeddings Generation: Use an embedding model (e.g., OpenAI’s text-embedding-ada-002) to transform each chunk into a vector.

5.2 Querying and Retrieval

  • User Query: “I want to learn about R Studio and notebooks.”
  • Create Query Embedding: Convert the user’s text into a vector with the same embedding model.
  • Cosine Similarity / Nearest Neighbor: Compare the query embedding to the stored vectors; return the chunks with the highest similarity scores.
  • Ranking & Display: Present the top relevant chunks (including links/time-stamps, if available).

5.3 Example Workflow

  • Dataset: YouTube transcripts of AI Show episodes, chunked and embedded.
  • Pandas DataFrame: Holds chunk text, embedding vectors, and any metadata (e.g., video timestamps).
  • User Prompt: “Search for R Studio and notebooks.”

Lesson 16: Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) and Vector Databases

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Bethan Chumba: AI Cloud Advocacy Team, Microsoft

1. Introduction

Welcome to Lesson 16 of our Generative AI for Beginners curriculum! As you’ve learned, large language models (LLMs) can produce remarkable outputs but often have limitations—such as limited knowledge of current events, lack of personal/proprietary data, or risk of hallucinations.

That’s where Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) comes in. By combining user queries with relevant data stored in a vector database (or another knowledge base), you can enhance LLM responses with factual grounding and context.

Key Focus: Understand why RAG addresses LLM constraints, learn about key components (like vector stores), and see how to build a full RAG application.

2. Why RAG Matters

LLMs trained mostly on public datasets struggle with:

  • Up-to-Date Facts: Might not know recent events or real-time data.
  • User-Specific or Proprietary Data: E.g., internal documents, store pricing.
  • Hallucinations: Generating text not tied to real evidence.

RAG solves these by retrieving relevant information and “augmenting” the prompt, ensuring the LLM relies on factual data.

Scenario Example

A user asks: “Is Queen Elizabeth II alive?” The base LLM (trained before 2022) might incorrectly say “Yes.” A RAG pipeline references current knowledge, returning a more accurate answer.

3. RAG Components

3.1 Knowledge Base

  • Stores relevant documents or data chunks (e.g., product catalogs, transcripts).
  • Information is typically embedded into vectors.

3.2 Retrieval System

  • Finds relevant chunks from the knowledge base based on user query.
  • Appends these chunks to the prompt, providing context to the LLM.

3.3 Vector Databases

  • Store numeric embedding vectors capturing semantic meaning.
  • Enable similarity search: quickly finding text chunks similar to the query.

4. Building a Full RAG Application

4.1 Prepare the Knowledge Base

  1. Gather Documents: PDFs, transcripts, Word files, etc.
  2. Chunking: Split long documents into smaller pieces for better retrieval and lower token cost.

4.2 Embeddings

  1. Convert Chunks to Vectors: Use an embedding model (e.g., text-embedding-ada-002).
  2. Store in Vector Database: Store vectors in a DB like Azure Cosmos DB (with vector indexing) or Cognitive Search.

4.3 Retrieval & Ranking

  1. User Query → Embedding: Convert the question to a vector.
  2. Similarity Search: Compare query vector to chunk vectors, find top matches.
  3. Re-rank (Optional): Use a second-stage ranker for relevance refinement.

4.4 Augment Prompt & Generate

  1. Combine: User query + top retrieved chunks → single prompt.
  2. LLM Call: Send the augmented prompt to the LLM.
  3. Response: LLM output merges the query with knowledge from your data.

5. Implementation Flow

Sample procedure for a “neural networks” knowledge base:

  1. User Input: “What is a perceptron?”
  2. Create Embedding of Input: Convert text to query vector.
  3. Search & Similarity: Find chunks about perceptrons in the vector DB.
  4. Augment Prompt:
    Question: What is a perceptron?
    Context: [Chunk text about perceptrons...]
  5. LLM API Call: Get a higher-fidelity answer based on provided context.
  6. Response Synthesis: e.g., “A perceptron is a foundational element of neural networks that…”

6. Evaluating Your RAG Solution

6.1 Groundedness

  • Check if the final output truly comes from the provided chunks. Avoid fabrication outside the context.

6.2 Relevance

  • Ensure retrieved chunks genuinely match the question. Good embeddings and ranking help.

6.3 Coherence

  • The final answer should be fluent and logical. Disjointed chunks can lead to choppy text.

7. Assignment

  2. Framework Exploration: Try a library/service simplifying RAG (e.g., built-in vector search). Compare with building from scratch.

Lesson 18: AI Agents

Frameworks, Design, and Use Cases for Autonomous Systems

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Corey Sterpače: AI Cloud Advocacy Team, Microsoft

1. Introduction

Welcome to Lesson 18 of our Generative AI for Beginners course! In prior lessons, you explored text-based chat, function calling, image generation, RAG, and more. Today, we examine AI agents—dynamic systems that combine an LLM, internal state, and tool access to accomplish tasks automatically or help users interact with external resources.

Key Focus: Understand what AI agents are, how agent frameworks implement them, and when they become valuable in building complex generative AI applications.

2. What Exactly Are AI Agents?

In this lesson, we use a broad definition:

  1. Large Language Model (LLM): Interprets queries, reasons, decides actions/tools.
  2. State: Tracks context/memory (history, partial solutions).
  3. Tools: External functions/APIs (database query, search, code execution).

Conceptually, the agent orchestrates the flow: User input → LLM decides tool → Tool executes → Agent processes results → Final user response.

3. Popular AI Agent Frameworks

3.1 LangChain

  • Core Idea: Combines LLM with a library of tools; maintains state/memory.
  • Defining an Agent: Specify LLM, tools, prompt/chain; agent decides tool calls.

3.2 Autogen

  • Multi-Agent Approach: Multiple LLMs with different roles (e.g., "Coder" vs. "Product Manager").
  • User Proxy: Mediates instructions, ensuring correct function/role handles them.
  • Tool Specification: Model autonomously picks functions to fulfill queries.

3.3 Task Weave

  • Code-First Agent: Config-driven approach for LLM and plugins.
  • Planner: Interprets requests, decides which plugin/function to invoke.
  • Plugins: Specialized tasks (anomaly detection, random numbers, etc.).

4. When Should You Use an AI Agent?

4.1 Complex Workflows

  • If your app requires multiple steps (search DB → call API → summarize), an agent can orchestrate it.

4.2 Tool-Rich Environments

  • Beneficial if the LLM needs to autonomously pick from many tools or data sources.

4.3 Autonomy vs. Control

  • Might be overkill for single function calls.
  • Shine when reasoning is needed: “Step A fails, try Step B’s tool…” or combining tools sequentially.

5. Design Considerations

  1. Security/Constraints: Add guardrails or explicit system instructions to prevent tool misuse.
  2. State Management: Decide how to store/fetch conversation history for context.
  3. Tool Catalog: Provide metadata for each tool (name, schema, output, constraints).
  4. Testing & Iteration: Evaluate tricky scenarios (partial results, bad data) for graceful recovery.

6. Assignment

  1. Experiment with a Framework: Choose LangChain, Autogen, or Task Weave. Implement a simple scenario (agent picks between search and math tool).
  2. Multiple Tools: Provide at least two calls (e.g., weather API, knowledge base). Observe how the agent chooses/sequences them.
  3. Reflection: Document which felt most intuitive. Did the agent handle edge cases or need custom prompts?

Key Takeaways

  • AI agents orchestrate complex tasks using an LLM, state, and tools.
  • Frameworks like LangChain, Autogen, and Task Weave handle memory, multi-LLM setups, or plugin definition differently.
  • Agents excel in multi-step/multi-domain workflows or when autonomous tool selection is needed.
  • Design includes security, state tracking, tool catalogs, and robust prompts.

Lesson 19: Fine-Tuning Large Language Models

Customizing Foundation Models for Specialized Tasks

🎙️ Meet Your Presenter

Nian Aran: Senior AI Advocate, Microsoft

1. Introduction

Welcome to Lesson 19! You've harnessed prompt engineering, RAG, and agents. Now, we explore fine-tuning—retraining an existing foundation model on your data to tailor it for a specialized task or style.

Key Focus: Understand why fine-tuning might benefit your application, when it’s most appropriate, and how to get started with data preparation, training, and evaluation.

2. What is Fine-Tuning?

Fine-tuning refines an LLM with domain-specific data. While a base model excels at general tasks, a fine-tuned model is optimized for a particular use case.

Foundation Model vs. Fine-Tuned Model

  • Foundation Model: Broad training, good for wide-ranging queries.
  • Fine-Tuned Model: Additional training on curated data; excels at specialized tasks, vocabularies, or styles.

3. Why Fine-Tune?

  1. Response Quality: Achieve consistency/style prompt engineering or RAG can't match.
  2. Reduced Token Overhead: "Bakes in" examples, avoiding costly few-shot prompts.
  3. Cost-Efficiency: Sometimes cheaper to run a smaller fine-tuned model than a large one with complex prompts.
  4. Task-Specific Flexibility: Advanced control over persona, formatting, domain knowledge.

However, evaluate simpler strategies (RAG, robust prompts) before committing to training overhead.

4. When Should You Fine-Tune?

  1. High-Impact Specialization: Unique domain (e.g., legal/medical jargon) or style needs.
  2. Exceeding Prompt/Token Limits: Large context examples frequently push constraints.
  3. Bottlenecked by Generic Models: Advanced prompting still doesn't deliver needed specificity/consistency.
Caution: Fine-tuning can be complex and time-intensive. Prepare data well, evaluate robustly, and plan for maintenance.

5. The Fine-Tuning Process

5.1 Data Preparation

  • Sourcing & Quality: Identify domain-specific examples covering task variety.
  • Format: Convert records to line-by-line JSON or JSONL.
  • Volume: Aim for 50–100+ quality samples for real tasks.
  • Fairness & Balance: Ensure coverage to avoid bias.

5.2 Create & Execute Fine-Tuning Job

  • Identify Fine-Tunable Model: Check provider docs for supported base models.
  • Upload Data: Send JSONL file to the service (Azure OpenAI, OpenAI).
  • Launch Training: Trigger job specifying base model and data.
  • Monitor: Watch logs/dashboard for progress and errors.

5.3 Evaluate & Deploy

  • Testing: Query new model with typical/edge cases; compare to baseline.
  • Iterate: Refine data or training parameters if needed.
  • Hosting: Deploy model as an endpoint; call using custom model name.

6. Demo Summary (Toy Example)

Scenario: Chatbot answering periodic table questions in limerick form.

  1. Create Training Data: 10 JSONL examples pairing query ("Tell me about gallium") with desired limerick response.
  2. Upload & Train: Submit data to fine-tune job via code/UI.
  3. Check Logs: Track job status.
  4. Compare: See custom model's playful output vs. generic model's direct answer.

7. Best Practices & Trade-Offs

  1. Verify RAG & Prompt Approaches First: Fine-tuning might be unnecessary.
  2. Manage Hosting & Maintenance: You own the custom model's upkeep.
  3. Update Strategy: Plan for re-training if base model updates or domain evolves.
  4. Monitor Performance: Continuously gather feedback for accuracy and bias.

8. Assignment

  1. Data Curation: Outline/gather 10+ specialized Q&A pairs (e.g., "company policies," "product specs").
  2. Fine-Tune & Evaluate: Use a notebook/environment to fine-tune a base model. Compare outputs to the original.
  3. Reflect: Did you see improvements? Evaluate cost/complexity for production.

Key Takeaways

  • Fine-tuning customizes LLMs for specialized tasks or formatting.
  • Measure overhead (data prep, cost, hosting) against simpler techniques (prompting, RAG).
  • Process: Prepare data → Train → Evaluate → Deploy.
  • Continuously monitor performance and plan for model maintenance/re-training.
By leveraging fine-tuning judiciously, you can transform a general-purpose model into one finely tuned for your domain—unlocking new depths of accuracy and personalization.

End of Lesson 19

Fine-tuning offers powerful customization but requires careful consideration of data, cost, and maintenance. You now understand when and how to apply this technique to enhance your generative AI applications for specific needs.
